Tiêu đề gốc: He knew which lottery tickets would win, without scratching them

This man could look at a lottery ticket, still unscratched, and tell you if it was a winner. The lottery checked his work. He was right 19 times out of 20.

Toronto, 2003. Mohan Srivastava is a geological statistician. His day job: estimate how much gold is buried in a mountain from a handful of rock samples. Finding patterns in noise is literally his profession.

A friend gives him a scratch ticket as a joke. He wins three dollars. And walking to claim it, one thought won't leave him alone: these tickets are mass produced. A computer decides exactly how many winners get printed. And whoever controls the winners cannot leave everything to chance.

The ticket carries eight tic-tac-toe boards: 72 visible numbers. Srivastava ignores what the numbers are. He counts how often each one appears. Most numbers repeat. A few appear exactly once. He calls them singletons.

Then he sees it. The singletons are almost always among the numbers hidden under the scratch layer. Three singletons in a row on one board means that ticket is a winner, about 90 percent of the time.

The lottery had built the leak itself. To print a fixed number of winners, one program had to design both the numbers you can see and the prize you can't. The design gave the game away.

He does the math on getting rich: standing in stores, sorting tickets, might earn him 600 dollars a day. Less than his consulting work. So he calls the lottery and warns them. They brush him off.

So he buys 20 tickets, sorts them unscratched into winners and losers, and mails them in. Two hours after the package arrives, the lottery calls back. Nineteen out of 20, correct. The next day, the game is pulled from every store in Ontario.

He could have stayed silent and skimmed winners for years. Instead, he used his crack exactly once: to prove it existed. Admirable. But if a flaw like that was hiding in one game, how many are still hiding in the others?
