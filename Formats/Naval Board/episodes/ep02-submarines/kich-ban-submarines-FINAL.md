# How Submarines Actually Fight — FINAL

## VOICEOVER (English)

140 sailors. No windows. More than 800 feet down. It can't see. It can't be seen. So how does a submarine find, and kill, anything?

It listens. Radar does not work through seawater. A boat can rise and raise a periscope, but that means leaving the deep and showing a mast. So the whole fight comes down to one thing: sound. And the rule is brutally simple. Whoever hears first, wins.

So how do submarines actually fight? In this video, we go through the old idea it all comes from. The two ways of listening, and why one of them can give you away. How a boat stays hidden for months. Where it fits around a carrier, and what drones are changing. And we finish with the deadly mistakes: four real cases where the silence broke.

The idea is more than 500 years old. In 1490, Leonardo da Vinci wrote down a trick. Stop your ship, put a long tube in the water, and put your ear to the other end. You will hear ships far away. Every submarine today is still doing that, with much better ears.

The weapon came later. On 17 February 1864, the Confederate submarine Hunley sank the sloop USS Housatonic with an explosive charge on a long spar. It was the first warship ever sunk by a submarine. The Hunley was lost the same night, with all eight of her crew.

Fifty years on, the idea grew teeth. On 22 September 1914, a small German boat, U-9, put a torpedo into the British cruiser Aboukir. The British assumed she had hit a mine, so two more cruisers closed in to rescue survivors. U-9 sank them too. Three cruisers in about an hour, and roughly 1,400 men lost.

That is the oldest lesson down here. If the target cannot work out where you are, even its best instincts can help you.

Now the modern version. In seawater, sound moves at about 1,500 metres a second. That is more than four times faster than in air. And in deep water it does something strange. It bends downward, turns, and comes back up to the surface about every 30 miles, out to several hundred miles.

That is why nobody can give you one number for how far a sonar reaches. It depends on the water, the speed and the target. The ocean decides what you get to hear.

Navies built on that. During the Cold War, the US Navy laid chains of hydrophones on the seabed, cabled to stations on shore. They ran along the western Atlantic, from Nova Scotia to Barbados. The ocean itself had been given ears.

There are two ways to use it. Passive sonar only listens, and gives nothing away. Active sonar sends out a pulse and reads the echo. That goes back to 1914, when Reginald Fessenden's echo ranger found an iceberg about two miles away. Think of a flashlight in a dark room. You can see the room, but now the room can see you too.

So a quiet boat would rather stay passive. But the US Navy itself now says passive sonar is no longer enough against modern quiet submarines. The boats have become so silent that hunters are pushed into pinging, and pinging gives the hunter away.

Which makes noise the real currency down here. A boat's machinery makes noise. The water flowing over its hull makes more, and that climbs quickly with speed. The Seawolf class is publicly credited with a top speed of 35 knots. Running silent, that drops to about 20. Its builder claims that at 25 knots, a Seawolf is harder to detect than an older boat tied up at the pier. Speed makes you loud, and it makes you deaf, because your own noise drowns out your sonar. Remember that. It comes back in the mistakes.

Quiet is worth so much that it has caused scandals. In 1987, the Pentagon announced that the Soviet Union had bought propeller-milling machinery from a Toshiba subsidiary. It warned this could make Soviet boats harder to track. Japanese experts disputed how much it really changed.

So how fast and how deep? Publicly, these boats are credited with more than 25 knots. And more than 800 feet. The real limits are classified.

Staying hidden starts with the ballast tanks. To dive, the boat opens its vents and lets seawater flood in. To rise, compressed air blows that water back out. Do that fast, and it is called an emergency blow. Hold on to that one.

Then there is air. A reactor does not need any, so a nuclear boat stays down. It even makes its own oxygen, splitting desalted seawater with electricity. So the limit is not air. It is food, with roughly 90 days stocked aboard.

A diesel boat has it harder. It must raise a snorkel mast to run its engines and recharge its batteries. That is its noisiest, most exposed moment. Some newer boats carry an extra engine that needs no outside air, and stay down for about two weeks at slow speed.

Which brings us to the carrier. A strike group can include up to two attack submarines, screening it against hostile ships and submarines. Where they are is rarely made public. It is hunter against hunter.

But the screen can be beaten. In a mid-2000s exercise, one small Swedish boat, Gotland, reportedly slipped through Carrier Strike Group 7 undetected. It reportedly came back with pictures of the USS Ronald Reagan. It was only an exercise, and that is exactly why navies run them.

In real war, the picture is harsher. On 2 May 1982, the British submarine HMS Conqueror fired three torpedoes at the Argentine cruiser General Belgrano. Two hit, and 323 people died. Her two escorts did not know what was happening. It was only the second ship sunk in action by any submarine since the Second World War.

And it is not history. On 4 March 2026, an American attack submarine torpedoed and sank the Iranian frigate Dena off Sri Lanka. Sri Lanka's navy pulled 32 survivors from the water. More than eighty of her crew never came home. It was reported as the first American submarine torpedo kill since the Second World War.

Now the hunted have a new problem too. In December 2025, Ukraine claimed an underwater drone had hit a Russian Kilo-class submarine in the port of Novorossiysk. Russia denied any damage. British intelligence assessed that significant damage was likely. If that holds, even a harbour is no longer a hiding place.

So the system works, as long as the crew respects it. Here are the deadly mistakes.

Mistake one: sailing with a noise you already know about. In February 1945, the German submarine U-864 was off Norway with an engine that kept misfiring. Aboard HMS Venturer, an operator picked up a faint sound on the hydrophones. Historians think it was probably that fault. Venturer's captain did not ping, because a ping would have given him away. He fired four torpedoes, and the last one hit. All 73 aboard were lost. It is the only time on record one submerged submarine has sunk another.

Mistake two: hearing a contact, and misjudging it. On 9 February 2001, south of Oahu, USS Greeneville had civilian visitors aboard. She performed an emergency blow to show them what she could do. She came up and struck the Japanese vessel Ehime Maru, which sank within minutes. Nine of the 35 aboard died. The National Transportation Safety Board blamed poor communication among senior crew, and a failure to analyse the contact properly. Hearing is not the same as knowing.

Mistake three: outrunning your own senses. In October 2021, USS Connecticut was running submerged in the Indo-Pacific. Her speed had been raised from 16 knots to 24. Her aft depth sounder degraded at speeds above 16. She struck an uncharted seamount, and 11 sailors were injured. The Navy did not blame any one decision. It found an accumulation of errors in navigation planning, watch-team execution and risk management. Its verdict: "it was preventable." It was not the first time. In 2005, USS San Francisco hit a seamount at top speed, 525 feet down, and one sailor died. Other charts aboard had shown the danger.

And mistake four: two boats, both built to be silent. On the night of 3 February 2009, two nuclear missile submarines met in the Atlantic. Britain's HMS Vanguard and France's Le Triomphant collided at very low speed. The French commander thought he had hit a container. We could not find a published inquiry explaining it. France's defence minister offered his own answer: "These submarines are undetectable, they make less noise than a shrimp." When everyone is silent, listening finds nothing.

Different boats, different decades, same lesson underneath. A noise ignored. A contact misread. A boat moving too fast to sense what was ahead. And two boats that may have been too quiet to hear each other.

So a submarine is not hard to kill because it is strong. It is a steel tube full of people. It is hard to kill because it hears you before you hear it. The moment that flips, it is just another target. Whoever hears first, wins.
