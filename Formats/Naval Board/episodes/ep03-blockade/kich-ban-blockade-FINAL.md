## VOICEOVER (English)

Since July, 147 merchant ships sailing to or from Iran have turned around. Five that refused were disabled. That is according to US Central Command. No battle at sea. No fleet sunk. Just ships deciding not to go.

A blockade does not need to sink ships. It needs them to turn around.

So how does a naval blockade win without firing a shot? In this video, we go through the old rule every blockade still lives by. How a navy chooses where to draw its line. How that line is enforced, with boarding parties, lawyers and insurance. What happens when the coast fires back. And we finish with the deadly mistakes: four times a blockade failed, or turned on the side that drew it.

The rule is more than 150 years old. In 1856, the great powers signed the Declaration of Paris. It says a blockade only counts if it is effective. It must be held by a force strong enough to really stop ships reaching the coast. A blockade that exists only on paper is not a blockade.

Five years later, the United States tried it on a huge scale. In 1861, the Union set out to blockade the whole Confederate coast, roughly 3,000 miles of it. It started with about ninety ships. Only around forty were fit to fight. To buy time, the navy filled old ships with stones and sank them across harbour mouths.

So most ships that tried to run the blockade got through. Of about 1,300 foreign vessels, roughly 1,000 passed unharmed. In total, about 8,500 ships slipped into Southern ports during the war. Before the war, some 20,000 had docked in just five years. And the South's cotton trade fell by 95 percent. That is the trick. The blockade did not catch most ships. It stopped most ships from ever trying. Remember that. It comes back in the mistakes.

Where you draw the line matters as much as how many ships hold it. A close blockade parks warships right outside the enemy's ports. By 1914, that had become too dangerous. Mines, torpedo boats and submarines could pick off ships waiting off a coast.

So Britain chose a distant blockade. It controlled the exits from the North Sea to the Atlantic, far from Germany's own coast. German ships could still leave port. They just could not reach the ocean.

That is the real geography of a blockade: chokepoints. Narrow water that trade cannot avoid. And the most important one in the world is the Strait of Hormuz. In 2024, about 20 million barrels of oil passed through it every day. That is about a fifth of all the oil the world uses. And most of it has no other way out. A few pipelines bypass the strait. Everything else has to sail through it.

Holding a line takes three things. The first is the right words. In October 1962, American spy planes found Soviet nuclear missile sites being built in Cuba. The Joint Chiefs wanted air strikes, then an invasion. President Kennedy chose a middle course. On 22 October, he ordered the US Navy to draw a line at sea around Cuba. Its job was to stop more missiles arriving. A line at sea gave Moscow time to choose. Kennedy did not call it a blockade. He called it a quarantine. A blockade assumed a state of war. A quarantine did not. It was also narrower: it targeted offensive weapons, not every cargo. That wording helped win the backing of the Organization of American States. Moscow called it an act of aggression anyway.

The second is the boarding party. On 26 October, US sailors boarded the freighter Marucla, a Soviet-chartered ship bound for Havana. They found no offensive weapons, and let her go. Some ships heading for Cuba simply turned back at the line.

The third is money. Ships do not sail without insurance. Then the Houthis began firing at ships in the Red Sea. In early December 2023, war-risk cover cost about a hundredth of a percent of a ship's value. Within weeks it reached as much as one percent, for a single voyage. On a big ship, that adds hundreds of thousands of dollars. An insurer can close a sea before a single warship arrives.

But the Red Sea also showed something new. You no longer need a fleet to block a sea. A coastline and missiles can do it. Since November 2023, the Houthis have targeted more than 100 merchant ships. By mid-2025, they had sunk four. In the first two months, traffic through the Suez Canal fell by about 40 percent. Ships took the long way, around the southern tip of Africa.

The Black Sea saw the same fight. In 2022, Russia blockaded Ukraine's ports. Ukraine answered from the shore, with missiles and drones. Over time, Russia moved warships away from Crimea.

And now the Gulf. Since the war began in late February 2026, Iran has severely restricted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. From April, the United States has blockaded Iran's own ports, with a pause in early summer. Two blockades, one strait. In November, US Central Command extended the effort to Iran's land borders, to stop goods simply going around the sea.

The cost lands far away. In September, about 11 million barrels a day got through Hormuz, roughly half the pre-war flow. Members of the International Energy Agency agreed to release 400 million barrels from emergency stocks. It was the largest release in the agency's history. At the pre-war rate, that is about 20 days of Hormuz oil. Fuel is the sharpest edge. Before the war, Middle Eastern refineries shipped about 4.7 million barrels of products a day. In September, it was 1.9 million. The agency now warns that the diesel shortage could last well into 2027. By the end of September, the world's oil stocks were 509 million barrels below their pre-war level. These figures are from autumn 2026, and they are still changing.

So a blockade can work without a single shot. But it can also leak, or backfire. Here are the deadly mistakes.

Mistake one: a line that leaks. A blockade only works if captains believe it. The Union learned that early, when most runners got through. It still happens. A week into the US blockade of Iran, the US military said it had turned around more than 20 ships. Lloyd's List, the shipping journal, counted something else. At least 26 Iran-linked vessels had kept moving. The Pentagon called the report false. Lloyd's List stood by it. Either way, every ship that gets through tells the next captain to try.

Mistake two: hitting the neutrals. In 1915, Germany answered Britain's blockade with a blockade of its own, by submarine. Germany even placed notices in New York newspapers, warning Americans not to sail on the British liner Lusitania. On 7 May, a U-boat torpedoed her. She sank in eighteen minutes. Nearly 1,200 people died, including 128 Americans. That alone did not bring America into the war. After American protests, Germany held its submarines back for a while. But on 1 February 1917, Germany went back to sinking ships without warning. Two months later, the United States declared war. Sink neutral ships, and you make new enemies.

Mistake three: forgetting the shore. A blockading fleet has to sit somewhere, and the coast can reach it. The Moskva was a cruiser built around air defence, the flagship of Russia's Black Sea Fleet. In April 2022, two Ukrainian missiles hit her. She sank the next day. The fleet that was meant to close the sea ended up pulling back from it.

Mistake four: thinking it will be quick. Britain's blockade of Germany began in 1914. It stayed in force after the fighting stopped, into 1919. Germany's public health board blamed it for 763,000 civilian deaths. A study in 1928 put the number nearer 424,000. Around 100,000 more may have died in the months it continued after the armistice. Either way, the cost of a long blockade falls on people who never see a warship.

Different centuries, different seas, same lesson underneath. A line nobody believes. A neutral ship sunk. A coast left armed. And a war that was supposed to end quickly.

So a blockade is not won by sinking ships. It is won by the ships that never sail. The cargo that is never loaded. The voyage that is never insured. The captain who looks at the line and goes home. The law draws the line. The navy makes it real. The insurers make it expensive. The navy holds a line, and everyone else decides whether to test it. The moment captains stop believing in it, it is just a few warships on an empty sea. A blockade does not need to sink ships. It needs them to turn around.
